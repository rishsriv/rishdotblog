## Highlights

**Chapter 1 – The team**

- Getting a mixture of engineers and marketers for growth hacking – ideally rolled into one person – is super important for growth hacking in the internet age
- Product driven growth hacking — where you convert or re-engage free/low-tier users to pro/high-tier users — costs nothing and can lead to significant revenue increases

**Chapter 2 – Determining if you’re ready for a growth push**

- You must not move into a high-tempo growth push until you know that your product is a must have, why it’s a must have, and to whom it is a must have. Growth hacking should only be deployed after the product has been determined to be a must-have
- NPS scores and “how disappointed would you be if this service stopped existing” questions are a good way to find out if you’ve reached project market fit
- A/B test as much as you can to see if you can determine what drives customer preferences

**Chapters 3+4 – The process**

- Run growth experiments at a high velocity to figure out what works and what doesn’t
- Create dashboards and automated reports that make it easy for your team to figure out:
  - 1. How your company is performing
  - 2. How the experiments you run are performing
- Have clear, quantifiable indicators to understand what experiments are performing and what are not
- Think about what confidence interval you’re willing to accept. 95% CI (1 in 20 results may be wrong) or 99% CI (1 in 100 might be wrong)
- Have a clear list of indicators to decide what experiments to run first. One way to do this is to rate all experiments on 3 metrics – ease of implementation, impact, confidence in success – and then take the arithmetic mean of all 3 as an overall score

**Chapter 5 – The hook**

- Language Market Fit: How well does the way you describe your product resonate with your customers? (Emails, mobile notifications, within the product, ads)
- The first piece of text a consumer sees must send the right message fast
- A/B test the hell out of your headlines and CTAs
- Prioritise the channels which you are going to use to reach customers. 3 categories of these — viral/word-of-mouth, organic, paid. Have some metrics to quantify this prioritisation
- Always be looking for new and emerging options. They tend to have much lower CPMs and sometimes much higher engagement than existing options
- For viral, the metrics you should care about are payload (how much text in a message), frequency, and conversion rate

**Chapter 6 – Activation**

- Map all of the steps that get users to an “aha!” Moment
- Make funnel reports to discover drop off points for each step. Track referrers in funnel
- Look at differences in behaviour of those you converted and those who didn’t

**Chapter 7 — Retention**

- Is super important because you don’t have any cac, can upsell, and learn more about customers over time (thereby giving them a better service)
- High and stable retention is a sign of product market fit
- Do cohort analysis to figure out what is causing changes in retention
- Using habit-forming loops, where possible, to drive retention
- Increasing the perceived value of rewards leads to greater retention
- The promise of a new feature/new content coming soon can lead to greater retention. Example — HBO’s Rome was a flop, but because it was so expensive and hyped, HBO had near zero churn 2 months before the shows release

**Chapter 8 — Monetization**

- Optimize for Customer Lifetime Value, not always for a one-off purchase
- To analyze monetisation, map the entire customer journey
  - Identify barriers to generating revenue (such as friction in the payment process)
  - For retail companies, the 3 key screens are 1) the screen displaying items for sale, 2) the shopping cart, and 3) the payments page
  - For SAAS companies, 1) page explaining features, prices, and plans, 2) pages promoting add-ons and upgrades
  - For ads — all pages where company can display ads
- Recommendation Engines are an insanely powerful tool here
- Understand which customers are willing to pay for bells and whistles and which only want the basic features, and create plans accordingly. Also, it can often be a good idea to create plans for students and non-profits. They are unlikely to ever become premium customers, but you can generate some revenue from them this way
- Ask users to make small commitments upfront (like Amazon’s wishlist) to lead to larger purchases later
