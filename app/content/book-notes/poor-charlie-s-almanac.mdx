# Poor Charlie’s Almanac

*Books · Investing* · [Source](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/944652.Poor_Charlie_s_Almanack)

![](/notion/poor-charlie-s-almanac/untitled.png)

## Summary

A lot of wisdom in this book — though it’s often buried as a throwaway comment instead of being an in your face insight.

Some key takeaways were:

- Learn when a technological change benefits you and when it doesn't. “There are all kinds of wonderful inventions that give you as owners nothing except the opportunity to spend a lot more money on business that’s still lousy. Often, all the savings from a new technology will flow through to consumers because of industry dynamics”
- On the price of stocks. “Stocks are valued partly like bonds, based on roughly rational projections of producing future cash. But they are also valued partly like Rembrandt paintings, purchased because prices have gone up”
- On the ideas that kill you. "It’s not the bad ideas that do you in. It’s the good ones. If a thing is a bad idea, it’s hard to overdo. But if it’s a good idea with a core of essential and important truth, you can’t ignore it. Then it’s easy to overdo"
- On investing. "A few major opportunities, usually recognizable as such, will usually come to those who continually search and wait. Then, all that’s required is to bet heavily when the odds are extremely favourable, using resources available as a result of prudence and patience in the past ... When you invest, bet big. Don’t take 'initial positions' or make 'small, speculative investments' ... Be willing to 'prepay; with rigorous preparation"
- On changing your mind. "Be willing, even eager, to change your mind. Identify your mistakes and learn from them. Any year you don’t destroy one of your best loved ideas is a wasted year. When a better tool (idea or approach) comes along, swap it for your old, less useful tool"
- On persuasion and consensus building. “If you want to persuade – appeal to interest and not to reason ... You can cause enormous offense by being right in way that causes somebody else to lose face in his own discipline or hierarchy”

## Approach to learning and decision making

Being prepared on a few occasions in a lifetime when opportunities arise, and then acting promptly and in scale, will often improve the financial results of your lifetime

**A few major opportunities, usually recognizable as such, will usually come to those who continually search and wait – with a curious mind that loves diagnosing multiple variables. Then, all that’s required is to bet heavily when the odds are extremely favourable, using resources available as a result of prudence and patience in the past**

To do this, you need preparation, patience, discipline, and objectivity

Business and the economy are like an ecosystem. Businesses that specialise in a narrow niche can do well. The winning business goes ridiculously far in maximising a few variables – the the discount warehouses of Costco

## Multiple Mental Models Approach to Business Analysis and Investment

You must know the big ideas in the big disciplines and use them routinely. All of them, not just a few. Most people are trained in one model – and try to solve all problems that way. “To the man with a hammer, every thing looks like a nail”. That’s a dumb way of solving problems

Conduct a comprehensive analysis of both the inner workings of an investment candidate as well as the larger integrated “ecosystem” in which it operates

Just as multiple factors shape almost every system, multiple models from various disciplines are needed to understand that system

**Be willing, even eager, to change your mind. Identify your mistakes and learn from them. Any year you don’t destroy one of your best loved ideas is a wasted year. When a better tool (idea or approach) comes along, swap it for your old, less useful tool**

**Sometimes, the signals coming out of your models reinforce each other. If a business seems at critical mass (positively or negatively), invest heavily**

Most of the time, the signals will be conflicting. When this happens, weigh trade offs carefully

Accept reality and don’t delude yourself. Bad things happen to those that do

A successful investment career boils down to only a handful of decisions. Don’t buy or sell very often

**A portfolio of 3 companies is plenty of diversification. Commit a high percentage of your capital to individual “focused” opportunities – specially when starting out**

**If your are convinced of your analysis, swim against the popular opinion. Indefinitely if necessary**

Focus first on what you want to avoid. “All I want to know is where I’ll diet, so that I never go there”

Eliminate the unprofitable positions of the chess board, so you can free your time and attention on the more productive regions ⇒ circle of competence + what to avoid

Never be too confident. You’re dealing with highly complex systems where everything is interacting with everything else

**It’s not the bad ideas that do you in. It’s the good ones. If a thing is a bad idea, it’s hard to overdo. But if it’s a good idea with a core of essential and important truth, you can’t ignore it. Then it’s easy to overdo**

The psychological factors of human misjudgement are some of the most important mental models that can be applied to an investment opportunity. Always do a two-track analysis:

- What factors really govern the interest involved, rationally considered?
- What are the psychological influences when the brain is automatically forming conclusions in ways that can malfunction

Quickly eliminate the big universe of what not to do, follow up with a fluency, multidisciplinary attack on what remains, then act decisively when, and only when, the right circumstances appear

## Munger’s investment evaluation process

Judge the staying quality of the business (competitive advantage)

Look for more value in discounted cashflow than what you’re paying for

Move only when you’re at an advantage. Have the discipline to bet only when the odds are in your favour

“I’m no genius. I’m smart in spots, and I stay around those spots” – circles of competence

Treat financial reports and accounting with skepticism. Added factors are:

- current and future regulatory climate
- state of labor, supplier, and customer relations
- potential impacts of changes in technology
- competitive strengths and vulnerabilities
- pricing power
- scalability
- environmental issues
- presence of hidden exposures
- quality of management

You ideally want the moat widened every year

Try to calculate the intrinsic value of the whole business, and compare it with the price

A great business at a fair price > a fair business at a great price

Margin of safety depends on the price paid. It is large at one price, small at another price, and non-existent at a high price. Always have a margin of safety

The evaluation of a business is not just mathematical. It’s philosophical. Ultimately, a “feel” should emerge from it

Once you identify a good business opportunity, apply a filter before pulling the trigger:

- What are the current price, volume, and momentum considerations?
- What disclose timing or other sensitivities exist?
- Do contingent exit opportunities exist?
- Are better uses of capital currently or potentially available?
- Is sufficient liquid capital currently on hand or must it be borrowed? What is the opportunity cost of that capital?

Cultivate a habit of far more time to learning and thinking than to doing

**When you invest, bet big. Don’t take “initial positions” or make “small, speculative investments”**

**Be willing to “prepay” with rigorous preparation**
