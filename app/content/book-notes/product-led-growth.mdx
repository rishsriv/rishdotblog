# Product-Led Growth

*Books · Startups* · [Source](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/46033247-product-led-growth)

## Overview

**Sales-led growth**

Old marketing playbook was to create content; use landing pages to capture leads; and nurture those leads with automated emails until, one day, they converted into paying customers (or unsubscribed).

*Pros of a sales-led go-to-market strategy*

1. Ability to close high Lifetime Value (LTV) customers
2. Perfect for hyper-niche solutions where TAM doesn't justify PLG
3. Perfect for new categories where customer education is needed.

*Cons of a sales-led GTM*

"First $1M ACV customer forced us to get on the elephant hunting treadmill, and we’ve never been able to get off it. Our board, our employees, everyone expects us to only go after customers that were as large or larger than our first customer"

**Product-led growth**

New product-led growth mode is so start with a freemium or free trial product. By making it easy for people to experience the value of the product, you can transform it into a powerful customer acquisition model.

*Pros of Product-Led Growth*

You have an unfair advantage when it comes to brand recognition and enjoy access to a dominant growth engine and significantly lower CACs.

*Cons of Product-led GTM*

- Many ambitious entrepreneurs have tried and failed. Rob Walling, the previous CEO of Drip, offers a warning: “Freemium is like a Samurai sword: unless you’re a master at using it, you can cut your arm off.”
- there’s no battle-tested playbook. You need to figure out whether a free trial or freemium model will work for your business.

## Deciding b/w Sales-led Growth vs PLG depends on 4 factors

### GTM Strategy

- Do you want to offer the best solution for the lowest price? (Dominant strategy)
- Do you want to offer the best-customized solution for the highest price to underserved customers? (Differentiated strategy)
- Do you want to offer the simplest product for the lowest price to over-served customers? (Disruptive strategy)

#### Dominant Strategy?

The dominant growth strategy works great if you can do something much better than your market and can charge significantly less.

Freemium can work here, but only if your TAM big enough to support a freemium model and you can walk the thin line between giving too much and too little away

Does your product solve a specific job significantly better and at a lower cost than anyone else in the market? Can your user realize significant ongoing value quickly with little-to-no help from company personnel? Do you want to be the undisputed market leader in your category?

#### Disruptive Strategy?

The freemium model thrives in the disruptive environment. Keeping costs low draws in prospects using existing solutions. When you provide less value for a cheaper price.

### Ocean Conditions

Are you in a red- or blue-ocean business?

If you’re in a blue ocean and have a quick time-to-value in your product, use a product-led model. However, if your product is complex, start with a sales-led go-to-market strategy to educate your audience and create demand

If you're in a red ocean environment and go with a disruptive strategy, freemium can really shine

### Audience

Are you currently targeting people who can easily use your product and experience its value? What is your ACV for each customer? Is it high enough to justify a low- or high-touch sales model?

Disadvantages of a Bottom-Up Selling Strategy:

- Low initial contract size
- Many non-paying customers
- Significant investment
- Expertise shortage when you want to convert some customers to an enterprise plan

### Time-to-value

To create a successful product-led business, you need a quick time-to-value. New users need to be able to experience a key outcome in your product quickly and without any assistance.

Eliminate unnecessary steps in your onboarding.

## Align product pitch to its value

To do this:

1. Understand your value
2. Quantify and communicate the value of your product to users
3. Deliver on what you promise

### Understand your value

#### What outcome do people expect when they buy your product?

There are 3 reasons that people buy a product

1. Functional Outcome: the core tasks that customers want to get done
2. Emotional Outcome: how customers want to feel or avoid feeling as a result of executing the core functional outcome
3. Social Outcome: how customers want to be perceived by others by using your product.

You ideally want to understand all 3 outcomes of using your product, and align them to a value metric. A value metric is the way you measure value exchange in your product. It can be functional or outcome based

- Functional value metrics are “per user” or “per 100 videos.” Pricing scales around a function of usage
- Outcome-based value metrics charge based on an outcome, like how many views a video received or how much money you made your customer.

#### What Makes a Good Value Metric?

1. It’s easy for the customer to understand. When someone visits your pricing page, will they immediately understand what they’re paying for and where they fit in your packaging?
2. It’s aligned with the value that the customer receives in the product.
3. Grows with your customer’s usage of that value.

People often fall into the trap of user-based pricing without thinking about it too much. It can work, but only if most of the conditions below are true.

| Condition                                                                                  | Example            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------ |
| Each user receives differentiated value from accessing the product                         | LinkedIn Recruiter |
| The customer has a strong need to standardize their department or company on the platform  | Salesforce         |
| The product has network effects, where initial users want to collaborate and invite others | Slack              |
| Budget predictability and control is critical for your buyer persona(s)                    | DocuSign           |
| Buyer is less sophisticated and needs easy-to-understand pricing                           | Evernote           |
| Usage metrics in your industry have become commoditized or are becoming table stakes       | Github             |

### Communicate Your Value

#### Don’t overcomplicate your pricing page

If the pricing page can’t pass the five-second test (i.e. users understand which plan is right for them almost immediately) you’re hurting customer acquisition

Putting together a pricing page doesn’t have to be complicated. You need four elements to make it work:

- Value Metric: what is the metric that you're pricing on?
- Willingness to pay for all packages: are there consumers that are willing to pay for each of the packages
- Valued features: are you feature-gating effectively?
- Customer personas: can you pitch each plan to a predefined customer persona?

#### Structuring pricing plans

Even if you have a simple pricing plan, you may still run into the trap of giving away too much for free. Don’t create a free plan with no incentive to upgrade. By offering the majority of your product for free on a freemium model, you reduce the incentive for people to upgrade.

It’s also way too easy to give away too little. By doing so, you make it extremely hard for new users to see value in your product—the powerful, fun features are hidden behind closed doors.

Finding an acceptable price range is especially important because we want to avoid two disastrous consequences: We set our price too high and lose out on the majority of sales. We set our price too low and lose out on the majority of profits (while also hurting our brand, which appears “cheap”)

### Deliver on Your Value

Be ruthless about reducing friction. Even non-trivial steps like activation emails can crater your free-to-paid conversion rate.

Does the first product experience lead to a specific, relevant, meaningful “quick win”? Do tooltips and hotspots spur meaningful action in the product, not just point at buttons? Do social and directional cues indicate high-value behaviors? Are key task completions indicated with a success state, such as Mailchimp’s famous high-five?

## The Bowling Alley Framework

*Develop your straight line. Create a product bumper. Build a conversational bumper.*

### Map Out the Path

There are form fields that you don’t really need to ask people when signing up. There are required steps that first-time users don’t really need to complete right away. And, of course, there are steps that don’t really need to be there at all. **So label every step**, eliminate everything that's not necessary, and postpone everything that delayable until after the user has signed up and gotten the first taste of value from the product

### Develop product bumpers

These help users adopt the product within the application itself. These include:

- Welcome Messages
  - show user a sticky welcome message about what the product can do when after they have signed up
- Product Tours
  - with a focus on what the product can help people achieve, instead of its features
- Progress Bars
  - to show them how far they are from finishing something
  - protip: never start these off at 0 – will make people more invested in the task
- Checklists
  - same purpose as progress bars, but more useful for "optional" tasks
  - same protip: never start them empty. always have some kind of prefilled stuff so that users are invested in the task (I have started this off, so I *must* complete it)
- Onboarding
  - have an onboarding screen that clearly lists what your product is for and its current limitations (if any), so there is no gap between users' expectations and what your product can currently deliver
- Tooltips
  - For helpful tips about what each feature does. You can make these less prominent in the future
- Empty States
  - Don't just leave something as an empty state! Use empty states as an invitation for people to do a task

### Develop conversational bumpers

Conversational bumpers work to educate users, bring them back into the application, and eventually upgrade their account. These include:

- User Onboarding
  - Talk to users – specially early on – before onboarding them. Helps set expectations
- Emails
  - Send people emails to tell them that something new about what they were working on
- Push Notifications
- Explainer Videos
- Direct Mail

Because of how valuable monitoring these usage patterns has become to product-led businesses, they’re often referred to as “value metrics.”

**A value metric is the way you measure value exchange in your product**.

value metrics are the linchpin to successful execution of a product-led go-to-market strategy. Because you’re aligning your revenue model directly with your customer acquisition model.

For a video platform like Wistia, a value metric could be the number of videos uploaded.

For a communication application like Slack, a value metric could be the number of messages sent.

For a payment processing platform like PayPal, a value metric could be the amount of revenue generated for a merchant.

**there are two types of value metrics: functional and outcome based. Functional value metrics are “per user” or “per 100 videos.” Pricing scales around a function of usage. Outcome-based value metrics charge based on an outcome, like how many views a video received or how much money you made your customer.**

value metrics outperform feature differentiation with up to 75% less churn. Outcome-based value metrics take this a step further with an additional 40% reduction in churn.

**1. It’s easy for the customer to understand. When someone visits your pricing page, will they immediately understand what they’re paying for and where they fit in your packaging?**

**2. It’s aligned with the value that the customer receives in the product.**

what core components lead someone to experience a meaningful outcome?

**3. Grows with your customer’s usage of that value.**

If customers get incredible value from your product, charge them more—your product is worth it. On the flip side, if customers aren’t getting the full value from your product, charge them less.

Given that Slack’s value metric is the number of users you add to the messaging platform, it makes sense to charge per user. However, if you’re an enterprise with a large number of users, one of your biggest objections is not knowing how many people are actually going to use the platform. To combat this, Slack created the fair billing policy—you get charged only for active users.

How to find your value metric

Step 1: Subjective Analysis

Pull out a piece of paper and jot down everything you think could be a value metric. Once you have the list, run it through the value metric scratch pad.

Step 2: Data-Driven Approach

When analyzing usage patterns, it’s easy to focus on measuring your product data without segmenting your users. By doing so, it’s easy to optimize for everyone while creating a worse experience for your best users.

ask yourself these questions when analyzing your data: What do my best customers do regularly in the product? What do my best customers not do in the product? What features did my best users try first during onboarding? What similarities among my best users—demographics, team structure, ability—led to success?

For churned customers, ask: What were some of the main differences between their user journey and that of your best customer? Specifically, what activities were different? What outcomes did your churned users achieve and not achieve? Were these churned customers in your target market? Why did the majority of these customers churn?

Four Common Pricing Strategies

1. Best-Judgement Pricing This is much like it sounds. You and your team decide your price based on what you think is reasonable. Having a low sales month? Discount. Making too many sales but can’t support new customers? Increase prices. It’s simple supply and demand.

least effective pricing strategy because you rely on the collective experience of your team, and you make assumptions about what your buyer values and is willing to pay.

2. Cost-Plus Pricing Cost-Plus Pricing works when you calculate the cost of selling and delivering the product, then add a profit margin on top.

If you use cost-plus pricing, you’re most likely saying goodbye to the majority of your business’ profit potential.

3. Competitor-Based Pricing Competitor-based pricing benchmarks your pricing based on their data. It’s relatively simple if your competitors publicly show their pricing.

Regardless of how you conjure up your competitor’s pricing info, you’re assuming that you sell to the exact same customer who has the exact same problem that you can solve with your exact same product.

you’re assuming that your competitor has done their customer and pricing research, which isn’t necessarily the case.

4. Value-Based Pricing Value-based pricing bases your price on the value you provide. You determine this by taking into account how prospects value your product. A significant benefit of value-based pricing—and, as part of the process, doing pricing and customer research—is that you’ll learn what to include in each package.

Functional Outcome: The core tasks that customers want to get done From a functional perspective, what does your product help people do? Typically, a functional outcome breaks down into factors such as incremental revenue, reduced cost, reduced risk, or time savings. In my experience, many SaaS founders have a good handle on the functional outcome of their product—they often created their solution to solve for their own problem.

Emotional Outcome: How customers want to feel—or avoid feeling—as a result of executing the core functional job Do people really like to “crunch the numbers”?

B2B buyers may, in fact, be even more emotional than their B2C counterparts.

Social Outcome: How customers want to be perceived by others Instead of a chart cobbled together in Microsoft Excel, Marko’s software helps you put together brilliant, professional reports that you can show to your executive team. You aren’t seen as the Excel guru anymore (which is replaceable). Because you didn’t spend an entire week crunching numbers, you had the time to put together a plan of attack for how you’re going to hit your targets.

a very good way to determine your price is to follow the 10x Rule. “We charge this much because our customers get at least 10x that much value.” If I sell something for $100, I want to provide at least $1,000 in value to them… at least. All we can do is get it AS RIGHT AS POSSIBLE out of the gate and understand that pricing is not a “set it and forget it” function, but – just as your overall Marketing Strategy is ever-evolving and changing with market forces, market feedback, etc.

We set our price too low and lose out on the majority of profits (while also hurting our brand, which appears “cheap”).**

If the price you were thinking of is below what an average buyer considers to be “acceptable,” then you’re leaving money on the table. If the price is above what an average prospect considers “expensive”, then you’ll probably face adoption hurdles and need to work especially hard at proving the value.

**Putting together a pricing page doesn’t have to be complicated. You need four elements to make it work: Value Metric; Willingness to pay for all packages; Valued features; Customer Information.**

According to Kyle Poyar, there are only three main categories when bundling: Leaders “are the hamburger in your McDonald’s value meal; they are what everyone wants and comes to you to buy. These must be included in all packages.” Fillers “are the fries and coke. They are seen as nice-to-have and sweeten the deal. Customers will cherry pick fillers when sold a la carte, and so a bundle helps drive uptake and a higher average revenue per user (ARPU).” Bundle killers “are the coffee of your value meal. Few people want a value meal with a burger, fries, coke AND a coffee, and adding coffee to the value meal might even turn people off from buying entirely because they’d end up with more than they need. There will be a handful of caffeine-starved customers who do want the coffee, though, and they can purchase

a la carte outside of the value meal.”

**Deliver on Your Value**

The same thing applies when selling software. What we promise in our marketing and sales is the perceived value. What we deliver in our product is the experienced value. Ideally, the perceived value aligns with the experienced value.

People want to “try before they buy” and experience your value proposition. If you keep your word, it’s a great way to build trust and sell your product. If you fail to deliver, your user experiences a nasty value gap.

**There are three main reasons why value gaps are so prominent in the SaaS industry: Your product has serious ability debt; You don’t understand why your customers buy; You overpromise what the solution is capable of.**

The Three Value Gaps You Need to Crush Value Gap 1: Ability Debt

Ability debt is the price you pay every time your user fails to accomplish a key outcome in your product.

**To chip away at your ability debt, you need to be ruthless about reducing friction. Even non-trivial steps like activation emails can crater your free-to-paid conversion rate.**

Ask yourself these questions while going through your product experience: **Does the first product experience lead to a specific, relevant, meaningful “quick win”? Do tooltips and hotspots spur meaningful action in the product, not just point at buttons? Do social and directional cues indicate high-value behaviors? Are key task completions indicated with a success state, such as Mailchimp’s famous high-five?** Are all unnecessary points of friction and distraction removed from critical workflows?

**Value Gap 2: You Don’t Understand Why People Buy Your Product.**

When it comes to onboarding, most businesses walk you through the entire product but bring you no closer to achieving a meaningful outcome in the product.

When you understand why people use your product, you can catapult them to the right destination. If you don’t know the primary outcome behind why people use your product, you’re growing your business on hard mode.

**Value Gap 3: You Suck At Communicating Your Value**

**If someone signs up for your product thinking that it’s a live-chat solution when it’s actually a telemarketing solution, you’re going to confuse users.**

This hurts your brand and increases the chances of new users leaving your initial product experience.

In your first meeting with the new trialer, do several things: Qualify them as you usually would. If they’re a good fit, let them dive into using the product

Ask them about the primary outcome they want to achieve with the product. Record these calls and use video so that you can see their body language—they might not tell you that your product is confusing as hell, but a fiery eye roll as you explain how “simple” it is will.

Watch the trialer try to achieve an outcome in the product. Ask your trialer to share their screen.

Do not show your trialer how to do everything. You want to see your trialer struggle. If you don’t let them struggle, the customer won’t remember how to accomplish that particular outcome, nor will you learn about your product’s shortcomings.

**The Bowling Alley Framework**

To master the Bowling Alley Framework, you need to do three things: **Develop your straight line. Create a product bumper. Build a conversational bumper.**

most users never make it to Point B—that promised land where they experience the value of your product. Why? Most often, we don’t know the desired outcome people are looking for, the reason they signed up to use your product.

**Map Out the Path**

**There are form fields that you don’t really need to ask people when signing up. There are required steps that first-time users don’t really need to complete right away. And, of course, there are steps that don’t really need to be there at all.**

sign up for your product and complete all the steps it takes to accomplish a meaningful outcome.

**Label Every Step**

Label each step throughout your onboarding experience using the colors green, yellow, or red: Green is absolutely necessary. Ex. Uploading a piece of JavaScript to your website; asking for an email address to setup an account.

Yellow is for advanced features that can be introduced later. Ex. Setting up a custom signature for your email address; running split tests on your video thumbnails.

Red can be removed completely. Ex. Changing the color of your profile picture; asking for someone’s nickname when setting up their account.

One of the reasons I love the straight-line system is because there is no bullshit. You will learn that your users simply need to complete steps X, Y, and Z for you to deliver on your product’s value.

1. Welcome Messages

The message explains why the founder created the product and restates the value proposition. Welcome messages can also increase a user’s motivation for using the product by clearly explaining the value and building suspense.

2. Product Tours

If you have a multi-product business, using a product tour at the beginning of your onboarding can be a gamechanger. You catapult people into the areas of the product that they care most about.

if you have a complex product with features that accomplish different tasks, a product tour is a must.

recommend using a “focus mode” that hides background elements to minimize the initial number of choices. Such product tours are extremely effective because they leverage Hick’s Law (decision time increases with every additional choice) and the Paradox of Choice (more choices make people less likely to choose).

3. Progress Bars Progress bars indicate how far a user has come, and how far they need to go.

Humans are addicted to progress and will work hard to finish something once they think it’s achievable. One caveat: Goals need to seem realistic. Just think of how demoralizing it would be to sign up for an account only to see a progress bar with “0 out of 99” steps completed.

Effective progress bars start with a substantial percentage of the bar already filled. This helps users feel like they’re already underway instead of starting from scratch, and it increases the desire to complete the task.

4. Onboarding Checklists Checklists break down big tasks into bite-sized ones.

you want to get the most out of your onboarding checklists, have them partly filled out by the time the user sees them. This simple tactic employs the “endowed progress effect”40 —people who think they’re close to completing something are more likely to see it through.

onboarding checklists also work well because of the Zeigarnik Effect,42 our tendency to think about incomplete tasks more than completed ones. Not finishing a task nags at us. Researchers call this “task tension.” Only completing the task can relieve it.

5. Onboarding Tooltips Onboarding tooltips help users learn how to use a product. They can reduce the burden on support and scale usability. Here are the main ways you can use hotspots and action-driven tooltips: Show first-time users how to use the product. Offer helpful tips to new users. Think of this like coaching. Show experienced users new areas of the product they might never have tried otherwise. This is great for increasing retention.

6. Empty States Upon first login, most software applications are boring. There’s no data specific to you;

For ConnectHero, this could simply be showing people the steps they need to take to complete their account setup.

To decide what to include in an empty state, ask yourself: What steps does a user need to complete to experience a quick win? What is the most important step in my straight line? How can I make sure that the majority of users complete this step?

Takeaways: Empty states are useful for when a user first lands on a product’s dashboard. Empty states should prompt users to take an action that will lead them closer to experiencing meaningful value in the product.

Better-Life Emails Better-life emails communicate the benefits of the product. The main call to action in these emails is often to upgrade an account. But you can also direct people to try specific features.

A common mistake with better-life emails is focusing only on the functional outcome of your product. Remember: You need to account for functional, social, and emotional outcomes.

Before writing your better-life emails, ask yourself these questions: When talking to potential buyers, what benefits get them most excited? What benefits make it a no-brainer for people to upgrade?

Better-life emails showcase the benefits of your product. The call to action can ask users to upgrade or help users experience product benefits for themselves.

Expiry-Warning Emails

Expiry-warning emails remind the user to upgrade before a free trial ends. Freemium models don’t use expiry-warning emails because part of the product is free forever.

If you have an opt-out free trial46 (you require a credit card upfront), you must send expiry-warning emails. If you don’t, you’re guaranteed to annoy users and flood your support reps with refund demands.

Customer-Welcome Emails

To reduce that anxiety, use customer-welcome emails: Reassure users that they made the right decision. Remind users of what they can do with the platform. Set expectations for what comes next (e.g. Will a customer success rep reach out?).

Too many businesses don’t welcome new customers immediately. Most reach out manually, but it takes time. In the interim, new customers become nervous, wondering if they made the right decision. Don’t make your new customer think twice. Remind them why they made the right decision.

Post-Trial Survey Emails Even with the most amazing free-trial experience, most users won’t convert. Some aren’t a good fit. Some might have not have had time to check out your tool. Others, well, they might have needed more time or someone to help them learn to use the product.

There are countless reasons, but you’ll never learn understand them if you don’t implement post-trial survey emails.
