## Summary

> 💡 This summary is focused around the early stage journey, because that’s what I’m most interested in. The actual interview has way more stuff!

Initial hunch for Retool founders: could there be level way of building internal software, like an Excel with higher order primitives?

Initially thought it would be for 2-3 person companies.

**Really fast iteration cycles**

Customer #2 said “Retool looks cool, but I don't have a database. I only have APIs. Can I connect Retool to my APIs?”. They built that integration overnight and sent it to the user the next day.

That user got to “happy” within maybe a week or two – it wasn't instantaneous. But they tried to make users as happy as possible

**Adding really granular analytics and alerts to call customers as soon as they faced a bug**

At the beginning, they had very few customers. Added very granular analytics (all built in-house, in order to capture data from adblock users as well) so they had a good sense of what people were doing with the product.

Also set up a Slack webhook that would notify them anytime a user was doing something in the product on Slack. Whenever they were pinged, they would look at live logs. And then set up sentries so that anytime any error occurred, they would immediately reach out to the customer. “You know, we might even call them actually, or we might even text them, if we have their phone number.”

“from a customer experience perspective, their experience was really quite intimate in the sense that, you know, using this product, you run into any error, immediately the CEO calls you, you know, a minute later or something like that.”

“it also taught us so much about the product, because we immediately sort of learned what the sort of major buggy areas were, for example, when they were using the products, what was friction full and what we should actually go and fix, how we should prioritize the product, what the use cases were, et cetera.”

**Outbound as a powerful way to get feedback**

Got their first 5 customers by pitching in person (”outbounding-in-person”). But the next few customers came via email outbounds.

Outbounding allowed them to iterate on messaging and understanding what the exact pain point was. “When we were talking with early customers, we would say things that were pretty out there just to sort of see what people thought about it.”

Iterating with the messaging, iterating with the product, iterating with the use cases, that was what the early customers helped us with. Sometimes we think of that as a language market fit.

“If you get a warm intro by an investor, for example, obviously, we'll take the call and they'll say, yeah, yeah, sounds interesting. This sort of warm intro is going to be quite dangerous, basically, because they sort of give you false optimism or false hope.”

They quickly realized that “Excel with higher order primitives” was a bad way to describe the product,

**Figuring out product features that scaled**

As a result of doing outbound, realized that their value proposition was very consistent but the use cases were very different.

So they building block features basically that could be used by a lot of customers rather than any one off feature.

Got enterprise customers very early on.

**Figuring out what was not their market**

One early hypothesis: “Retool as a product actually is kind of similar to something like a file maker, for example, or a Microsoft Access or a Visual Basic. And so what we should do is actually we should go find customers of FileMaker and try to convince them to use Retool”

That seemed like a plausible path to find a product market fit. So David went to LinkedIn, basically found a sort of FileMaker user groups, and infiltrated these groups. Sent a few hundred cold emails, but only got a one percent reply rate. The FileMaker developer that we talked to basically said, this is a horrible idea. I cannot believe you're working on this idea. Like FileMaker is great.

So they realized that they should look outside the Access/FileMaker universe.
